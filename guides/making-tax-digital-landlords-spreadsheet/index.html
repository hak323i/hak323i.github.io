Making Tax Digital for landlords: a simple record-keeping spreadsheet that fits

Making Tax Digital (MTD) for Income Tax started on 6 April 2026 for the first group of landlords and sole traders. If your rental income is heading towards the thresholds, the easiest win is to keep your records in HMRC's categories from day one.

Checked against GOV.UK: check if you need to use MTD for Income Tax and GOV.UK: send quarterly updates on 10 October 2026.

Who has to join, and when

Qualifying incomeIn tax yearMTD from
Over £50,0002024–256 April 2026
Over £30,0002025–266 April 2027
Over £20,0002026–276 April 2028

"Qualifying income" is your gross income from self-employment and property added together, before expenses. A landlord with £24,000 of rent and £8,000 of self-employed income has £32,000, even if their profit is far lower.

What you'll have to do

  • Keep digital records of income and expenses.
  • Send a quarterly update. Each one is a running total of income and expenses by category for the tax year so far, not a tax return.
  • Submit your tax return by 31 January after the tax year ends, as now.

You must still send an update for a quarter with nothing in it. GOV.UK says there are no penalty points for late quarterly updates in 2026–27, but that relaxation doesn't apply to the tax return.

Can I keep using a spreadsheet?

Yes, but not on its own. GOV.UK says you need software that works with MTD. A spreadsheet can stay as your record if you use "bridging" software that connects to it and sends the figures to HMRC. Pick one from HMRC's list; HMRC doesn't recommend a particular product. The spreadsheet's job is to keep tidy totals in the right categories.

Use HMRC's categories

Match your columns to the categories on the property pages of the tax return, so the totals drop straight in:

  • Rents and other income from property
  • Rent, rates, insurance and ground rents
  • Property repairs and maintenance
  • Legal, management and other professional fees
  • Costs of services provided, including wages
  • Other allowable property expenses
  • Costs of replacing domestic items
  • Residential property finance costs (mortgage interest, which gets a tax credit rather than a deduction)

Worked example: one quarter

A landlord with one flat let at £950 a month records this for 6 July to 5 October 2026:

DateDescriptionCategoryAmount
Jul–SepRent, 3 monthsRents and other income£2,850.00
12/07/2026Agent's fees (July–September)Legal, management and professional fees£342.00
03/08/2026Replace leaking kitchen tapRepairs and maintenance£145.00
20/08/2026Landlord insuranceRent, rates, insurance and ground rents£260.00
Jul–SepMortgage interestResidential property finance costs£1,215.00

The update due by 7 November covers 6 April to 5 October, so these figures are added to the first quarter's totals before sending. A simple SUMIFS by category and date range does it.

Tips

  • Record each item when it happens, with a short description you'll understand next year.
  • Keep receipts and invoices; a phone photo in a dated folder works.
  • Split repairs (allowable) from improvements (generally capital) as you go.
  • If your gross property income is £1,000 or less, the property allowance may mean you don't need to report it.
