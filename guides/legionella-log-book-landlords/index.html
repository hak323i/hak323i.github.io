Legionella log book for landlords: what to record and how often

A legionella log book is simply the place where your risk assessment, your checks and any fixes live together. Here's how to set one up for a buy-to-let or HMO, what HSE guidance expects, and what a filled-in month looks like.

Sources: HSE: Legionella and landlords' responsibilities, ACOP L8 and HSG274 Part 2. Checked 10 October 2026.

Do you actually need a log book?

Every landlord has a duty to assess the risk from legionella, even when renting out a room in their own home. HSE is clear that for most homes this doesn't need to be an in-depth assessment. A typical low-risk property has mains-fed cold water with no storage tank, a combi boiler or other low-volume heater and regular daily use. A simple assessment may show no further action is needed, and HSE says testing (sampling) the water is not usually required for domestic properties.

A log book becomes much more useful when the risk is higher, for example:

  • a hot water cylinder or a cold water storage tank in the loft;
  • an HMO with several bathrooms and showers, some of them used rarely;
  • a property that sits empty between tenancies;
  • communal systems in a block you manage.

Even in a low-risk flat, writing down the date of your assessment and what you checked shows you've done what's required.

What to put in it

  1. Front page: property address, who is responsible for managing the risk, and contact details.
  2. Risk assessment summary: date, who did it, main findings and the next review date. Review it when something changes, such as a new boiler, new tenants or a long void.
  3. Asset list: cylinder, tanks, showers, thermostatic mixing valves (TMVs) and little-used outlets.
  4. Sentinel outlets: the nearest and furthest taps from the cylinder or tank, so the same ones are checked every time.
  5. Monthly temperature log: the reading, how long it took to get there, pass or fail, and initials.
  6. Weekly flushing log for outlets that aren't used at least weekly.
  7. Remedial actions: what failed, what was done and when.
  8. Maintenance: showerhead cleaning, tank inspections and TMV servicing.

How often (where your risk assessment calls for monitoring)

Your risk assessment may set different frequencies. Follow it and note why.

The failure isn't the problem; an unrecorded failure is. Writing down the cause, the fix and the re-test is what makes the log worth having.

Void periods

HSE highlights extra steps for properties left empty: water stagnates, so flush the system before new tenants move in. For longer voids, arrange for someone to run all outlets regularly, and log each visit.

How long to keep it

L8 says to keep records for at least five years. A spreadsheet backed up to the cloud is easier to share with an agent or inspector than a damp notebook in the meter cupboard.
