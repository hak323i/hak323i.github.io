What to put in a one-page profile or communication passport

A one-page profile or communication passport helps someone new get to know a person quickly: how they communicate, what they're good at and what helps. It's useful when starting a new class, club, college or job, or before a hospital or GP appointment. The best ones are written with the person, in their own voice wherever possible.

One-page profile or passport?

A one-page profile is a short summary, often three headings: what people like and admire about me, what's important to me, and how best to support me. A communication passport is longer, with a page for each topic. Many people have both: the one-pager on top for a quick read and the passport for more detail.

What to include

  1. About me: name, what I like to be called, a photo, and a few lines about who I am.
  2. Strengths and interests: start here, not with difficulties. "I know every bus route in Leeds." "I'm brilliant with animals."
  3. How I communicate: speech, AAC, signing, pictures, typing, writing, gestures or scripts. All ways of communicating count. Include how to tell when I mean yes or no.
  4. How to communicate with me: "Use my name first." "Give me time to answer; I'm thinking." "Write it down if it's important."
  5. What helps me: routines, warnings before changes, a quiet space, movement breaks.
  6. Sensory: what I find hard (strong smells, buzzing lights, crowds) and what helps.
  7. When things get too much: signs to look out for, please do, please don't, and what I might need afterwards.
  8. Food and drink, toilet and personal care: only what's needed for that setting.
  9. Important people: who to contact and who I trust.
  10. Health information: optional. The person (or their family) decides what's included.

Example wording

Instead of...Try...
"Has challenging behaviour when routines change.""I find sudden changes hard. Please tell me early, and show me a picture of what's happening instead."
"Non-verbal.""I don't use speech much. I use my communication app and gestures. Thumbs up means yes."
"Won't eat lunch.""The dinner hall is very loud for me. I eat better somewhere quieter."
"Refuses to do PE.""Changing rooms are hard for me. I can join in if I can change somewhere private first."

The second column says what helps, not just what goes wrong, and it's in the person's voice. That's what makes a passport useful to the next person who reads it.

Tips

  • Keep each page short. Bullet points are fine.
  • Review it at least once a year, and whenever something big changes.
  • The person decides who sees it. Mark any page that shouldn't be shared.
  • Keep a printed copy in a school bag or hospital bag, and a digital copy on your phone.

A passport is not a medical record and doesn't replace an EHC plan, school SEN support plan or NHS documents. For how SEN support works in schools, see GOV.UK: extra help for children with SEND. This is general information, not medical or professional advice.
