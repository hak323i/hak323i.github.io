Land sourcing and option deals explained

Land with planning permission can be worth many times more than the same land without it. Land sourcing is about finding sites with that potential, agreeing terms with the owner and getting them through planning, or passing them to someone who will. Here's how the main deal types work, with a worked example of who gets what.

Step 1: finding sites

  • Look at the council's local plan and any call for sites. Land next to existing settlements, or allocated for housing, is a common starting point. Check GOV.UK on planning permission for how the system works.
  • Check ownership with HM Land Registry. Title registers and plans cost a few pounds each.
  • Note constraints early: flood zones, access, green belt, heritage and protected habitats.
  • Keep a log of every site, owner, contact and follow-up date. Most deals take many conversations over months or years. If you hold owners' personal data, follow data protection rules.

Step 2: the main deal structures

Option agreementPromotion agreement
How it worksThe buyer pays the owner a fee for the right to buy the land within a set period, usually at market value less an agreed discountA promoter funds the planning application, then the land is sold on the open market and the proceeds split
Who buysThe option holderA third party, often a housebuilder
Owner's rewardOption fee plus the agreed priceAn agreed share of the sale price, after agreed costs
Main risk for the ownerThe discount or price termsTiming; it relies on a sale

Both need a solicitor on each side. Tax can apply too. For example, HMRC treats the grant of an option over land as a separate land transaction for SDLT (see HMRC's SDLT manual). Take professional tax advice.

Step 3: estimating the uplift

A quick first look uses one of three methods: value per plot, value per net developable acre, or a simple residual (gross development value minus build costs, fees, finance and developer's profit). Use more than one and see whether they roughly agree.

Worked example (all figures illustrative)

A 5-acre field on the edge of a village is worth about £60,000 as farmland. With outline planning for 35 homes, a housebuilder might pay around £50,000 per plot, so £1,750,000.

Option (15% discount)Promotion (owner gets 80% net)
Sale value with planning£1,750,000£1,750,000
Planning and promotion costs£150,000 (paid by option holder)£150,000 (deducted before the split)
Owner receives£1,487,500 (85% of value) plus option fee80% of £1,600,000 = £1,280,000
Other party's gross gain£262,500 discount, less £150,000 costs = £112,500 before resale costs20% of £1,600,000 = £320,000, after recovering its £150,000

The structure changes who carries the risk. If planning is refused, the owner keeps the field and the option holder or promoter has lost their costs. Always model the refused scenario, not just the good one.

Common mistakes

  • Valuing the site at headline plot values without allowing for affordable housing, infrastructure and planning obligations.
  • Forgetting how long planning takes. Annualised returns look very different over five years than over one.
  • Losing track of owners and follow-ups. Most sites are won by the person who keeps in touch.
