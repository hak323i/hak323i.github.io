How to analyse a UK buy-to-let deal in 10 minutes

Most deals can be ruled in or out with six numbers and a calculator. This is the quick first pass I'd run before booking a viewing, with a worked example and a free spreadsheet tab that does the sums.

The six numbers you need

  1. Purchase price
  2. Realistic monthly rent (check let, not asking, prices for similar homes nearby)
  3. Deposit percentage and mortgage rate
  4. Buying costs: stamp duty, legal fees, survey and any broker or lender fees
  5. Any work needed before letting
  6. Running costs: management, insurance, maintenance, voids and safety certificates

Worked example

A two-bed terraced house in England for £180,000, likely to let at £1,000 a month, bought with a 75% interest-only mortgage at 5%. The buyer already owns a home, so the higher stamp duty rates apply.

Step 1: stamp duty

Using the higher rates on GOV.UK: 5% of the first £125,000 (£6,250) plus 7% of the next £55,000 (£3,850) = £10,100. Our stamp duty guide explains the bands.

Step 2: cash you put in

ItemAmount
Deposit (25%)£45,000
Stamp duty£10,100
Legal fees and survey (estimate)£2,500
Lender fee (paid upfront)£1,000
Light refurbishment£3,000
Total cash in£61,600

Step 3: yearly running costs

ItemPer year
Letting and management (12% of rent, including VAT)£1,440
Landlord insurance£300
Maintenance allowance (5% of rent)£600
Voids (about two weeks a year, 4%)£480
Gas safety check and EICR (averaged)£150
Total£2,970

Step 4: the key figures

MeasureSumResult
Gross yield£12,000 ÷ £180,0006.7%
Net yield (before mortgage)(£12,000 − £2,970) ÷ £180,0005.0%
Mortgage interest£135,000 × 5%£6,750 a year
Cash flow before tax£12,000 − £2,970 − £6,750£2,280 a year (£190 a month)
Return on cash before tax£2,280 ÷ £61,6003.7%

Step 5: stress test

Re-run the cash flow at a higher rate. At 7%, interest rises to £9,450 and cash flow becomes −£420 a year. That doesn't automatically kill the deal, but it tells you how much buffer you need when the fixed rate ends.

Lenders run their own test, usually that rent covers interest by a set margin at a stressed rate. As an illustration, at 145% cover and a 5.5% test rate, £12,000 of rent supports a loan of about £150,000 (£12,000 ÷ (1.45 × 5.5%)), so the £135,000 loan here would pass. Each lender's numbers differ, so check with a broker.

Don't forget tax

If you own in your own name, mortgage interest isn't deducted from rental profit. Instead you get a tax credit at the basic rate (20% in 2026/27), and GOV.UK confirms the property basic rate and the credit rise to 22% from 6 April 2027. Higher-rate taxpayers therefore keep much less of that £2,280. See GOV.UK: working out your rental income.

Quick verdict rules of thumb

  • Cash flow that goes negative at a rate only 2 percentage points higher is fragile.
  • A rent figure from one agent's valuation isn't evidence. Look at what has actually let.
  • Maintenance and voids are real costs, even if a year passes without them.
